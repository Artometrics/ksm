## 01. Capes and Hats

Narrator (Kore): [SILENCE] [HARD] Madrid, March 1766. The king's minister, the Marquis of Esquilache, has banned long capes and broad-brimmed hats, partly to modernize the city and partly so criminals cannot hide under them. Times are already hard. The city riots.
Narrator (Kore): King Charles the Third orders an inquiry. Its council decides the Jesuits were behind the riots. The evidence for that is thin. On February twenty-seventh, 1767, Charles signs a decree ordering the Jesuits out of Spain and its empire. The decree invokes public order, then adds, in translation,
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): other urgent, just, and necessary reasons, which I reserve in my royal mind.
Narrator (Kore): In New Spain, the viceroy set June twenty-fifth, 1767, for the operation against the Jesuit houses. In Baja California, a desert peninsula of scattered missions, carrying it out took months longer. The officer sent to carry it out was Gaspar de Portolá. Storms held him up. He reached the coast in December. The missionaries were spread so thin that he could not gather them at Loreto until February 1768. Then they sailed into exile.
Narrator (Kore): Their missions went to the Franciscans, under Junípero Serra. The king's inspector general, José de Gálvez, had a separate plan for the northern frontier: send soldiers and friars, by land and by sea, to occupy San Diego and Monterey. Spanish officials feared rival powers advancing on the coast.
Narrator (Kore): The expulsion brought Portolá to Baja California and put the old Jesuit missions in Franciscan hands.

## 02. The Expedition

Narrator (Kore): July fourteenth, 1769. Sixty-four men, by the standard count, leave San Diego for Monterey with a string of mules. Spanish soldiers, two Franciscan friars, an engineer, Christian Indians from the Baja missions, and their commander, Portolá.
Narrator (Kore): On October tenth, near today's Watsonville, the friar who kept the most detailed diary, Juan Crespí, describes very high trees of a red color, not known to them. Because nobody recognizes them, he writes, they are named redwood, from their color. It is generally cited as the first European written description of the coast redwood.
Narrator (Kore): They are looking for Monterey. They walk past it without recognizing it.
Narrator (Kore): Saturday, November fourth, 1769. It is the feast of San Carlos Borromeo, the saint they came north to honor at Monterey, and the name-day of the king. They say Mass on the beach and eat mussels off the rocks. Then they climb into the hills, on the ridge now called Sweeney Ridge, and from the top they see what one of them calls an immense arm of the sea. A day later, Portolá's own diary records the view flatly. Before them
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): extended a great arm of the sea, sixteen to twenty leagues
Narrator (Kore): in extent.
Narrator (Kore): The bay was not empty. Along the lower San Francisquito Creek watershed, occupation sites date back more than five thousand years. The Puichon were an independent Ramaytush Ohlone tribe with villages in this watershed. The peninsula held many distinct Native communities, now known collectively as Ohlone. Older books say Costanoan. Malcolm Harris writes that the Ohlone moved
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): in response to abundance rather than scarcity.
Narrator (Kore): Acorns ripening, fish spawning, birds arriving on the marshes: short bursts of hard work, then long stretches of what Harris calls
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): social luxury.
Narrator (Kore): On November sixth they make camp beside that creek. Whether the surviving redwood called El Palo Alto is the tree they camped under remains disputed. The diaries confirm five nights by the creek, but do not settle which redwood it was. Malcolm Harris puts the camp under it. Palo Alto historian Steve Staiger thinks it may have been another redwood, farther downstream, cut down long ago.
Narrator (Kore): After a council of officers decides to turn back, the expedition starts south on November eleventh. On the way home they still do not recognize Monterey. Portolá comes back by the next year and founds the presidio there on June third, 1770. He sails away on July ninth.
Narrator (Kore): This is Cardinal. Episode one: The Ledger.

## 03. What a Mission Was

Narrator (Kore): What a Mission Was.
Narrator (Kore): In 1773, Pope Clement the Fourteenth formally suppresses the Jesuit order.
Narrator (Kore): In 1774, Father Francisco Palóu puts up a wooden cross near a big tree on San Francisquito Creek and marks the spot as a possible mission site.
Narrator (Kore): [HARD] August fifth, 1775. A small Spanish supply ship called the San Carlos feels her way into San Francisco Bay. The current runs so hard that the sounding lead will not reach the bottom: the water is carrying it away. The San Carlos is the first European ship recorded sailing into the bay. Her commander is Lieutenant Juan Manuel de Ayala. His pilot, José de Cañizares, takes the launch and helps chart the bay. They anchor by an island Ayala names Isla de los Ángeles. Angel Island.
Narrator (Kore): In March 1776, the commander Juan Bautista de Anza and Father Pedro Font pass the creek on their return from San Francisco. On March thirtieth, Font measures the tree with a graphometer borrowed at Mission San Carlos: about fifty varas high. The creek is judged too unreliable to water mission fields through a dry summer. The tree is passed over. That same year a mission is founded up the peninsula, San Francisco de Asís, which almost everyone will come to call Mission Dolores.
Narrator (Kore): The mission for this valley goes farther southeast. January twelfth, 1777. On the bank of the Guadalupe River, Franciscan friars plant a cross and put up a shelter for worship. Misión Santa Clara de Asís, on Thámien land, the eighth mission in Alta California. Franciscan, not Jesuit.
Narrator (Kore): For almost five months, the mission records no baptisms. On June sixth, 1777, Father Tomás de la Peña goes out to two villages and baptizes twenty children who are sick. That same week comes a baptism inside the mission church: a boy about a year old, from near Coyote Creek. Father José Murguía records his parents' names and names the boy José María. He records the godparents too. Child, parents, village, godparents, the priest's name. All of it goes into the book.
Narrator (Kore): What those parents made of the baptism, the book does not say.
Narrator (Kore): A California mission was a church, a farm, a fort's partner, and a labor system. A baptized Native person became a neófito, a neophyte, a new believer. Native workers grew the wheat, tended the cattle, wove the cloth, tanned the leather, and rendered the tallow. Soldiers guarded the friars.
Narrator (Kore): Once baptized, you were expected to live at the mission, under the friars' rules. You were not free to leave. Santa Clara University's Ohlone heritage project puts it plainly. Native people from dozens of communities were brought to live together at the mission, and,
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): laboring without pay, these Native Californians built the mission structures and provided the agricultural and industrial workforce to support Spain's colony in California.
Narrator (Kore): They could not leave freely, they were not paid, and they could be physically punished or imprisoned.
Narrator (Kore): Serra died on August twenty-eighth, 1784, at Mission San Carlos in Carmel, when nine of the eventual twenty-one missions existed. His successor, Fermín Francisco de Lasuén, founded nine more between 1786 and 1798.

## 04. The Royal Road

Narrator (Kore): The Royal Road.
Narrator (Kore): Heavy freight came by sea, on supply ships from San Blas. Overland, soldiers and friars traveled on horseback and on foot, with Native workers and pack animals.
Narrator (Kore): There was no single El Camino Real. Camino Real just means royal road, and there were many of them. Native trade routes already crossed the country. The Autry Museum of the American West calls the idea of one mission-lined highway an American invention of the twentieth century.
Narrator (Kore): In 1814, Pope Pius the Seventh restores the Jesuit order.

## 05. Secularization

Narrator (Kore): Secularization.
Narrator (Kore): 1821. Mexico wins its independence from Spain, and the far-off province of Alta California becomes Mexican.
Narrator (Kore): The last mission was founded at Sonoma in 1823.
Narrator (Kore): In 1824, a Californio named Secundino Robles finds the red rock the Ohlone had long dug for pigment, south of Santa Clara. A search there for gold and silver comes to nothing.
Narrator (Kore): Many of the mission friars were Spanish-born, and disputes over allegiance to the new Mexican government divided them from officials. The missions controlled extensive farmland and Native labor. Mexican liberals wanted church power separated from the state. Soldiers and settlers wanted the land. Some officials believed the baptized Indians ought to be free citizens.
Narrator (Kore): The word is secularization, from the Latin saeculum, the world, as opposed to the cloister. To secularize a mission meant, on paper, to turn it into an ordinary parish under a bishop and to release its lands, herds, and people.
Narrator (Kore): It came in steps. On July twenty-fifth, 1826, Governor José María de Echeandía issued a proclamation that let qualified mission Indians in the southern districts leave mission rule and become Mexican citizens.
Narrator (Kore): In 1827, Santa Clara's recorded Native population is one thousand four hundred sixty-two.
Narrator (Kore): At Santa Clara in 1831, the records count twenty-one baptisms of children born at the mission, and eighty-two burials. Across the Bay Area missions, deaths outstripped births. New converts kept the populations from falling even faster.
Narrator (Kore): In 1832, Santa Clara's recorded population is one thousand one hundred twenty-five.
Narrator (Kore): On August seventeenth, 1833, the Mexican Congress decreed the secularization of the California missions. On August ninth, 1834, Governor José Figueroa issued the rules. They promised Native households plots of mission land and shares of the livestock, tools, and seed. They also required communal work on land still undistributed and barred the sale of allotted land and livestock. Santa Clara University's Ohlone heritage project puts the promise this way. The lands
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): were supposed to be held in trust by the Franciscans on behalf of Native people.
Narrator (Kore): These were promises of distributions, not a record that every household had received its share.
Narrator (Kore): In August 1834, Richard Henry Dana, a Harvard student whose eyes have been damaged by measles, signs on as a common sailor aboard the brig Pilgrim. The ship is bound around Cape Horn for California, to collect cowhides.

## 06. Silver and Hides

Narrator (Kore): Silver and Hides.
Narrator (Kore): In 1835, Dana is on the California coast. Of Monterey he writes,
LETTER (Puck: Richard Henry Dana, about twenty, a Harvard student turned common sailor, sardonic, sea-wind in the voice): "They have no circulating medium but silver and hides, which the sailors call California bank notes."
Narrator (Kore): The missions and the ranches ran enormous herds. There was no railroad and no bank. Fresh beef could not survive the voyage to Boston; hides and tallow could. Boston houses sent ships around South America with manufactured goods and swapped them for hides, which went home to become shoes. A hide was worth about two dollars. Rancheros paid their debts in hides.
Narrator (Kore): At San Diego, Dana's company had a hide-house with room for forty thousand of them, and the crew would stand in it calculating how long it would take to fill. At that local valuation, forty thousand hides is eighty thousand dollars.
LETTER (Puck: Richard Henry Dana, the young sailor, dry and exact): "Their hides, too, which they value at two dollars in money, they give for something which costs seventy-five cents in Boston; and buy shoes, like as not made of their own hides, and which have been carried twice around Cape Horn, at three or four dollars."
Narrator (Kore): And things sold, he wrote,
Quote (Charon, flat documentary): at an advance of nearly three hundred per cent upon the Boston prices.
Narrator (Kore): A cow is skinned in California. The hide becomes a shoe in Boston. The shoe sails back, and the person who raised the cow pays several times the Boston price.
Narrator (Kore): Dana describes one official custom-house, at Monterey, where trading ships had to enter their cargo before doing business on the coast. At Santa Barbara the ship anchored three miles offshore, and the town was nearly a mile farther inland. So far as the crew were concerned, Dana wrote, the town

## 07. The Rancho

Narrator (Kore): The Rancho.
Narrator (Kore): In 1839, Alexander Forbes, a merchant in Tepic, Mexico, publishes his book on California in London.
Narrator (Kore): In 1839, Governor Juan Bautista Alvarado grants Antonio Buelna land along San Francisquito Creek. Rancho San Francisquito.
Narrator (Kore): A grant began with a petition to the governor and a map of the land. Officials investigated the request. The governor could issue a título, a title, subject to approval by the departmental assembly. A local judge could then put the grantee in possession and measure the land. The map was called a diseño. Some were rough sketches, marking creeks, hills, and neighboring holdings.
Narrator (Kore): The boundaries were described through places people knew: creeks, neighboring holdings, the land belonging to a mission.
Narrator (Kore): At the end of 1839, Santa Clara's census records two hundred ninety-one Native residents. The census, signed December thirty-first, is still in the archives.
Narrator (Kore): In 1840, Dana publishes Two Years Before the Mast. At Santa Clara, the recorded Native population is three hundred forty-four. Historian Robert Jackson finds that deaths alone cannot explain the mission's shrinking headcounts. For the period ending in 1840, he estimates that about four hundred people either left the mission or were ranch workers baptized by its friars rather than residents.
Narrator (Kore): In 1841, two more grants appear along the creek. Rancho Rinconada del Arroyo de San Francisquito goes to María Antonia Mesa. The surviving land-case catalog records Governor Alvarado's grant of Rancho Rincón de San Francisquito to José Peña in March.
Narrator (Kore): On February fifteenth, 1844, Governor Manuel Micheltorena issues title to Posolmi, near the bay, to Lope Yñigo, an Ohlone man baptized at Mission Santa Clara.
Narrator (Kore): In 1845, Andrés Castillero identifies the red rock as cinnabar, the ore of mercury. Secundino Robles and his brother Teodoro receive a share.
Narrator (Kore): In December 1845, Governor Pío Pico sold mission lands at San Juan Capistrano to his brother-in-law, John Forster, and to James McKinley, for seven hundred ten dollars. The church and certain other buildings were excepted.
Narrator (Kore): In the years before the American conquest, some Native families obtained formal grants; others lived on Californio ranchos. The Muwekma Ohlone Tribe's history describes both, not an empty landscape.

## 09. Prove It

Narrator (Kore): Prove It.
Narrator (Kore): By 1850, Santa Clara's baptismal registers record more than eleven thousand baptisms. Over the mission era, Santa Clara recorded more baptisms and burials than any other mission in the chain. The pages still exist.
Narrator (Kore): California becomes a state on September ninth, 1850. On March third, 1851, Congress creates a commission to settle private land claims. People claiming land under Spanish or Mexican titles must present their claims and their evidence. A grant and a diseño are now documents to put before an American board.
Narrator (Kore): On March fourth, 1851, Bishop Joseph Alemany gives the parish at Santa Clara to an Italian Jesuit, John Nobili. Nobili opens a school on the mission grounds: Santa Clara College.
Narrator (Kore): In 1852, a young lawyer in Port Washington, Wisconsin, is preparing to leave for California. In a letter to his parents, he reports a fire that destroyed his library, papers, and account books. His plans depend on settling his affairs and finding the money for the journey. In July he reaches San Francisco. In the mining country he goes into business selling supplies to miners.
